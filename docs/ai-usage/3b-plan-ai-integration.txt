⏺ Mostly agree. The chess.com-style coached game is the right idea, and your
  code is already set up for it. I'd change a few things about how it's built,
  and I'd pick a different angle for puzzles. Here's what I found in the repo
  and what I'd do.

  What the repo already gives you

  - The engine is pure TypeScript in shared/. search.ts
    (shared/src/engine/search.ts) returns a score for every legal move.
    analyze.ts (shared/src/engine/analyze.ts) has classify() with the
    best/good/inaccuracy/mistake/blunder thresholds. motifs.ts
    (shared/src/engine/motifs.ts) explains why a move is good or bad (corners,
    X-squares, C-squares). The browser can run all of it, so coached practice
    needs no backend.
  - CoachBubble.tsx (frontend/src/components/CoachBubble.tsx) already has
    coachLine(ply), which turns a classification plus motifs into text. Practice
    mode can reuse it.
  - There's no bot opponent yet. You'd need to add one.

  AI Coach practice: agree, with five changes

  1. Grade moves with the engine, not the LLM. An LLM call on every move is too
  slow and costs money on each move. Rating a move is simple: look up the played
  move's score, then classify(loss), then detectMotifs, then the template in
  coachLine. The words the coach uses then match Game Review exactly. That
  consistency is a selling point: one engine and one vocabulary across Review,
  Practice and Puzzles. Keep the LLM for an optional "Explain why" button that
  reuses the other session's CoachService on a single position.

  2. Run the search while the player is thinking. When it becomes the user's
  turn, a Web Worker scores every legal move. By the time they click, the rating
  is already there, so feedback is instant and "Show best move" costs nothing.
  A worker matters because a search can take around 400ms and would freeze the
  UI on the main thread.

  3. Add a hint before the move, not only after it. Chess.com works this way
  too. Offer a ladder:
  - Hint: name the idea behind the best move, e.g. "a corner is available" or
    "watch the X-square".
  - Show best move: highlight the square.
  - Take back: after a bad move, undo it and try again.

  You learn more by finding the move yourself than by seeing it after a mistake.
  You could also track "found the best move on the 2nd try".

  4. Make the bot beatable. At full strength the engine will crush beginners,
  and they'll leave. Offer 3 levels using search depth plus randomness: easy
  picks randomly among the top 3–4 moves, capped at "mistake"-sized losses; hard
  plays the best move. This is a small pure function, say
  shared/src/engine/bot.ts, with a seeded random number generator so tests are
  repeatable.

  5. Pause the bot so takebacks stay simple. After the user moves, show the
  grade and wait for them to continue (or reply after about 1s if the move was
  best or good). If the bot hasn't moved yet, "undo" just removes the last move
  from the list. Keep the game state as moves[] in a reducer in shared/ that you
  can unit-test (play / undo / botMove / reveal), with no React involved.

  Open questions:
  - Should practice games be saved? For v1 I'd say no, since the requirement to
    save results covers human-vs-human games. Saving them later would feed the
    skill profile.
  - Should the eval bar show during practice? It gives the answer away, so make
    it a toggle that's off by default.

  Puzzles: build them from each player's own mistakes

  A static puzzle collection is just content, and incumbents win on content.
  Your advantage is that you already store engine analysis for every game. So:

  - "Your mistakes" puzzles. Every inaccuracy or worse by the player (the same
    filter selectKeyMoments in coach.ts (shared/src/coach.ts) uses) becomes a
    puzzle: "You played c4 here. Find the better move." These are personal, free
    to generate, and tie back to Review.
  - A quality rule for puzzles. Only accept positions where the best move beats
    the second-best by at least the mistake threshold (5 discs), so there's
    exactly one clear answer. Positions with 12 or fewer empty squares are
    solved exactly by the engine, so those puzzles are provably correct. That's
    a good claim for your design notes.
  - Tag each puzzle with its motif, like missed_corner or x_square. That tag
    later connects puzzles to the sample skill bars on LearnPage.tsx
    (frontend/src/pages/LearnPage.tsx): your weakest theme leads to puzzles on
    that theme.

  For v1, generate them on the fly from analysis you already have, with no new
  table. In the roadmap: a puzzles table and a puzzle_attempts table, a seeded
  shared set (a script run over stored games), multi-move puzzles, and spaced
  repetition for ones you got wrong.

  Scope for your remaining ~2 hours

  Your README says you have about 2 hours left for the edge. Both features won't

  1. Coached practice (~75 min): the bot, the reducer with tests, the engine

  3. Roadmap only: skill profile, saved puzzles and attempts, spaced repetition,

  Coached Practice: implementation plan

  What the player experiences

  1. From Learn, they choose Easy / Medium / Hard and a color (Black / White /
     Random).
  2. On their turn, the engine scores every legal move in the background. They
     can ask for a Hint (the idea behind the best move) or Show best (a ring on
     the best square).
  3. They play a move and get an instant grade in the coach bubble, e.g. "c4 is
     a mistake: gives away about 6 discs. f5 was best. This move opens a corner
     for the opponent."
  4. After a best or good move, the bot replies after about 1s. After an
     inaccuracy or worse, the bot waits until they choose Take back or Continue.
  5. Undo works at any point and always goes back to their previous turn.
  6. At game over they see a final score plus accuracy and move-class counts,
     using the same summarize() as Review. "New game" starts again.
  7. The eval bar toggle is off by default and remembered per browser in
     localStorage, wrapped in try/catch.

  No backend, no login, no saved games. The page never touches GameClient, so it
  also works in mock mode.

  Revised flow: the bot never blocks

  Take back already works after the bot has replied, because undo removes both
  the bot's move and the player's. So there's no need to stop the game for the
  player to decide. The bot always replies on its own, and the only thing that
  changes is how long it waits.

  After every move:
  1. The player's disc lands and the grade appears in the coach bubble straight
     away. The position was scored while they were thinking, so there's no wait.
  2. A short delay runs. Its length depends on the grade:

  ┌────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │       Grade        │            Delay before the bot replies            │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Best / Good /      │ ~0.5s (feels like a normal game)                   │
  │ Forced             │                                                    │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Inaccuracy         │ ~1.2s                                              │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Mistake / Blunder  │ ~2.5s, with a small progress ring on the Take back │
  │                    │  button                                            │
  └────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  3. The bot plays. The grade of the player's move stays in the bubble until
     their next move, so they can still read it after the bot replies.

  What the player can do at any moment (all optional):

  ┌───────────────┬─────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────┐
  │    Action     │ Before the bot replies  │    After the bot replies     │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┤
  │ Take back     │ Cancels the bot's reply │ Removes the bot's move and   │
  │ (button or ←) │  and removes their move │ theirs                       │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┤
  │ Show best /   │ Pauses the bot and      │ Shows the best move for the  │
  │ Hint          │ shows the answer        │ position the player is now   │
  │               │                         │ in                           │
  ├───────────────┼─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┤
  │ Nothing       │ The bot replies when    │ They play on                 │
  │               │ the delay ends          │                              │
  └───────────────┴─────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┘

  The only pause comes from the player. Clicking Show best or Hint while the bot
  is waiting to reply means they're studying the position, so the bot waits. In
  just that case, a Reply button appears (Space also works) to let the bot
  move. Someone who never asks for help never sees a pause or a button.

  Example timelines

  - Normal move: play f5, see "f5 is best", and the bot answers about 0.5s
    later. No clicks beyond the move itself.
  - Blunder, player moves on: play b2, see "b2 is a blunder: opens the a1
    corner. c4 was best", and the bot answers after about 2.5s. The player keeps
    going.
  - Blunder, player wants to learn: play b2, see the blunder grade, and click
    Show best within the delay. The bot pauses and a ring appears on c4. The
    player takes back and plays c4, which is graded "best", and the bot replies.
  - Player notices after the bot replied: the bot has already taken a1. The
    player presses Take back, which removes a1 and b2, and they're back at their
    turn to try again.

  Timing details to get right

  - The delay starts only once the grade is on screen. If the player moved
    before scoring finished, the timer waits for the grade, so the bot never
    moves before they've seen feedback.
  - The bot computes during the delay. Its reply appears when both the delay has
    passed and its search is done. On Hard, the search usually fits inside the
    delay, so it doesn't add waiting time.
  - Every timer and worker result is tied to the position. After a take back,
    any pending bot reply or late result for the old position is ignored, so the
    board can't jump after an undo.

  Changes to the earlier plan

  - Reducer: remove awaitingDecision, add paused: boolean (set by Hint or Show
    best while a reply is pending, cleared by Reply or Take back), and replace
    the continue action with resume.
  - Hook: add the delay-by-grade table (a REPLY_DELAY_MS table next to
    BOT_LEVELS), the cancellable timer, and the rule that waits for the grade
    before starting the timer.
  - Page: remove the Continue button; add the progress ring on Take back for
    mistakes and blunders; show the Reply button only when paused.
  - Tests: a pending reply is cancelled by Take back; Show best while pending
    pauses the bot and Reply resumes it; Take back after the bot replies removes
    two moves. The timer itself belongs in the hook, not the reducer, so the
    tests only need to cover the state changes.

  One possible later addition is a "Pause after mistakes" setting (off by
  default) for players who want the old behavior. I'd leave it out of this
  build.

  build because it reuses what is already there.

  ### Why puzzles, and why these puzzles
  A generic puzzle collection is content, and the incumbent wins on content. Our
  edge is coaching: **one engine and one vocabulary** across the product. Game
  Review diagnoses a game, Practice drills you live, and Puzzles close the loop
  by **turning your own mistakes into exercises**. "You played b2 here and gave
  away the corner. Find the better move." Every puzzle traces back to a game you
  actually played.

  ### What already exists that this builds on
  | Existing piece | What puzzles get from it |
  |---|---|
  | `moves` table, analyzed per ply (`classification`, `loss`, `best_square`,
  `candidates`, `motifs`, `is_exact`, `board_after`) | Every puzzle position and
  its answer are already stored. Finding candidate puzzles is a SQL query, not
  new engine work. |
  | `games.black_account_id` / `white_account_id` | "Your" mistakes = moves by
  the seat your account played |
  | `gradeMove` + the engine Web Worker (Practice mode) | Grades a puzzle
  attempt in the browser, with the same grades as Review and Practice |
  | `motifs` (x_square, allowed_corner, missed_corner, …) | Puzzle themes, for
  free |
  | `Board` (`bestSquare`, `ghost`), `CoachBubble`, Show best | The puzzle UI is
  mostly the practice screen without the bot |
  | Planned `POST /api/coach/explain` (see the Practice roadmap entry) |
  "Explain why" after a wrong answer, with no extra backend work |
  | The sample corner puzzle on the Learn page | Replaced by real puzzles |

  ### Design

  **Step 1: "Your mistakes" (next up, about half a day)**
  - **Selection:** your non-forced moves classified inaccuracy or worse, from
  finished, analyzed games, most recent first.
  - **Only puzzles with one clear answer:** keep a position only if the best
  move beats the second-best by at least the mistake threshold (5 discs, from
  the stored `candidates`). A puzzle with two nearly equal answers teaches
  nothing and feels unfair.
  - **Checking an answer:** accept any move graded `best` by `gradeMove` in the
  worker, not only `best_square`. If two moves are equally strong, the player
  isn't marked wrong for picking the other one. On a wrong answer, show the
  grade, allow a retry, then offer Show best.
  - **Trust labels:** positions with `is_exact` (12 or fewer empties, solved to
  the end) are provably correct and labelled "Solved". Midgame puzzles rest on
  the heuristic evaluation, which is why the clear-answer rule above matters.
  - **No new tables:** puzzles are computed on the fly from `moves`. Attempts
  are kept per browser only.

  **Step 2: stored puzzles and progress (Milestone 2)**
  - `puzzles (id, source_game_id, ply, board, player, answer_squares, themes,
  is_exact, gap, engine_version, created_at)`, unique on `(source_game_id,
  ply)`.
  - `puzzle_attempts (account_id, puzzle_id, solved, tries, used_hint,
  attempted_at)`.
  - **Generation:** the `AnalysisRunner` inserts candidate puzzles when a game's
  analysis finishes, plus a one-off script to fill them in for existing games.
  - **Resurfacing missed puzzles:** a simple schedule, missed puzzles come back
  after 1, 3, then 7 days.
  - **A shared puzzle set:** good puzzles from all public games, so new players
  have something to solve before they have a history.

  **Step 3: skill profile and training plan (Milestone 2–3)**
  - Motif-tagged attempts plus review motif counts become the skill bars on the
  Learn page, which are sample data today. "Build my plan" then picks puzzles
  and practice positions for your weakest themes.
  - **Puzzles longer than one move:** a sequence you must play correctly move
  after move, mostly from exactly solved endgames. This needs the search to
  return its principal variation (the engine's best line), which it doesn't
  today, since it only scores the moves in the current position.

  ### Open questions (left open on purpose)
  - **Guests:** puzzles from games played without an account? Seat tokens live
  in one browser, so guests' puzzles would be tied to that browser only.
  - **Difficulty:** per-puzzle ratings (like Lichess, which updates a puzzle's
  rating from player results), or a simple heuristic from the size of the gap
  and the number of empty squares? A rating is better but needs volume.
  - **Engine upgrades:** a stronger engine (see bitboards / tuned evaluation)
  could change a stored puzzle's answer. We store `engine_version` and plan to
  re-check puzzles when it changes, but we haven't decided whether an outdated
  puzzle is fixed or retired.
  - **Privacy:** games are public today, so mining puzzles from other people's
  games is allowed, but should they be anonymized by default?

  The Learn page shows a training plan and a set of lessons, but neither is
  built yet. The training plan uses sample data and its "Build my plan" button
  is disabled. Of the six lessons, only "The rules" works, and it links to the
  rules section on the home page. I chose to spend my time on Practice mode
  instead, because it gives a player something real to do today. Both pieces are
  designed to reuse what is already built rather than start over, and this
  section explains how.

  The training plan is the piece that ties the product together. Review tells a
  player what went wrong in one game. The plan looks across all their games,
  finds the pattern, and sends them to the lesson, puzzle or practice position
  that will help most. The incumbent has ratings and game history, but nothing
  that turns your history into a next step, and that is the gap this fills.

  Most of the data is already stored. Every analyzed move in the moves table has
  its classification, disc loss, motifs and whether the position was solved
  exactly. Accounts are linked to games through black_account_id and
  white_account_id. A skill profile is therefore a query over rows we already
  have, grouped by theme instead of by game. Corners would come from how often a
  player misses a corner, hands one over, or plays an X-square next to an empty
  one. Edges would come from C-square moves. Mobility would come from moves
  where the coach's fact sheet already says the opponent's number of replies
  explains the loss. Openings and Endgame would come from accuracy in those
  phases, using the same phase boundaries as the coach. The endgame score is
  especially reliable because most of those positions are solved exactly. Each
  score would use the same accuracy formula as Review, restricted to the
  relevant moves. Players would therefore see one consistent measure everywhere.

  The plan itself is then a simple rule: find the weakest theme with enough
  evidence, and suggest the matching lesson, puzzles taken from the player's own
  games in that theme, and a practice game. The engine and SQL choose what to
  recommend. An LLM could later write a short summary on top, in the same way
  the Review coach explains engine facts it is given rather than deciding
  anything itself. I would add the plan only after puzzles are stored in the
  database, because puzzles are the most useful thing it can recommend. Until
  then, it could link to the lessons and to Review moments tagged with the weak
  theme.

  Lessons are the most traditional part of the page, and on their own they are
  not an edge; any site can publish articles. What makes them fit this product
  is that they connect to everything else. Each lesson would be short, written
  by us, and interactive. It would use the same Board component with diagram
  positions generated from real games, the way the Learn page's sample puzzle
  already is. It would end with two or three positions where the player makes
  the move themselves, graded by the same gradeMove used in Practice. The lesson
  topics already match the engine's motif tags. So when a Review or Practice
  move is flagged as an X-square mistake, the coach can link straight to
  "Corners and X-squares". That link is cheap to build and makes the lessons
  feel like part of the coaching rather than a separate library. Lesson
  completion would be stored per account alongside puzzle attempts, so the plan
  knows what the player has already covered.

  I would write the lesson text by hand rather than generate it with an LLM.
  Wrong Othello strategy presented as a lesson would damage trust in the whole
  coaching product, and six good lessons are not much work. The positions inside
  each lesson can still be checked automatically with the engine in tests, the
  same way the sample puzzle's answer is fixed.

  Some questions I am deliberately leaving open. First, what a skill score
  should be compared against: early on, absolute accuracy per theme is all we
  can measure, but "you misplay corners more than players at your level" needs
  ratings and enough players to make the comparison meaningful. Second, how much
  history the plan needs before it is honest; my guess is around five analyzed
  games, but that should be tested. Third, whether guests get a plan at all,
  since their games are only tied to a seat token in one browser. Fourth, the
  openings lesson assumes named opening lines, and the engine has no opening
  book yet, so that lesson depends on the opening book already on the roadmap.
  Finally, like the analysis engine, the training plan is a candidate for a paid
  tier, and that ties into the pricing question already open in the Milestone 3
  notes.
